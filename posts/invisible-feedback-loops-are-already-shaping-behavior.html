Invisible feedback loops are already shaping behavior

May 3, 2026 · 5 min read · modern-l-d, work, strategy

An accountancy firm in the UK built a recruitment game to evaluate senior-manager candidates. The game had two layers: a technical assessment on audit regulations, and a behavioral layer measuring how candidates handled monotony, accuracy, and pressure. Candidates loved it. The firm's brand reputation jumped. But the designers discovered that several behavioral questions were so open to interpretation that candidates were going left when the hiring team expected right. The game was shaping hiring decisions - just not always the decisions anyone intended. That gap between the feedback loop you designed and the one actually operating is where most organizational interventions live or die.

Leaders invest in capability strategies - new frameworks, competency models, culture documents - and then watch execution quality stay flat. The assumption is that better strategy language will produce better behavior. It does not, because the behavior was never shaped by language in the first place. It was shaped by feedback loops that were already operating: what gets praised in meetings, what gets questioned in reviews, what happens to people who try something new and fail, what happens to people who play it safe and deliver adequately.

These loops are invisible in the sense that nobody wrote them down or approved them in a planning session. But they are perfectly legible if you watch what people actually do. A company that says it values creativity but promotes only people who follow process has a feedback loop that rewards compliance and punishes deviation. No amount of creativity workshops will override that loop, because the loop is delivering real consequences - promotion, money, status - every single day. The announced value is aspirational. The invisible loop is operational.

Baldwin and Ford's foundational research on training transfer identified this pattern decades ago: the work environment - specifically, the cues, consequences, and feedback that people encounter after training - determines whether new skills survive. The training itself is a minor predictor. The post-training environment is the major one. Organizations keep investing in the minor predictor and wondering why nothing changes.

Consider what happens when a manager tells their team 'I want you to take more ownership' while simultaneously rewriting every deliverable that crosses their desk. The announced loop says: take initiative. The operating loop says: your work will be corrected regardless, so why bother? The operating loop wins because it has a consequence attached to every single action, not just to the ones the manager happens to notice. The announced loop has a consequence only when the manager remembers to reinforce it, which is inconsistent and usually late.

Grossman and Salas's synthesis of transfer research confirmed this with specific mechanisms: skills transfer when the post-training environment provides deliberate practice opportunities, timely feedback, and supervisor support for applying new approaches. When any of these conditions is missing - when the supervisor corrects everything, when there is no time to practice, when feedback is annual rather than immediate - the new skill decays. The invisible loop (correct everything) overpowers the announced one (take ownership) because it is more frequent, more immediate, and more consequential.

The accountancy firm discovered this in miniature. Their recruitment game was designed to surface behavioral traits. But the phrasing of certain questions created an unintended loop: candidates who interpreted questions literally scored differently from candidates who read between the lines. Neither group was wrong. The game was rewarding a specific reading style that the designers had not intended to measure. The invisible loop - question phrasing → interpretation → score - was more powerful than the intended loop about behavioral traits.

Not every invisible loop needs redesigning. Some of the most effective organizational behaviors are sustained by loops nobody formalized. The senior engineer who quietly reviews every junior's pull request before merging, not because a process document requires it but because the team culture rewards care - that is an invisible loop producing quality. The team that organically developed a practice of debriefing after client calls, not because a manager mandated it but because one person started doing it and others found it valuable - that is an invisible loop producing learning.

The design challenge is not to replace all invisible loops with formal ones. It is to notice which invisible loops are producing the behaviors you want and protect them, while identifying which ones are producing the behaviors you do not want and disrupting them. The disruption does not require a new strategy document. It requires changing what happens immediately after the behavior - the consequence, the signal, the response. Change the consequence and the loop rewires itself.

Pick one behavior your organization keeps trying to change - faster decision-making, more cross-team collaboration, better written communication. Then map what actually happens in the 48 hours after someone performs that behavior. Do they get praised, questioned, ignored, or corrected? Does the behavior make their next task easier or harder? Does anyone else notice? Track this for five instances across two weeks. You will likely find that the consequence attached to the desired behavior is either absent, delayed, or negative - while the consequence attached to the undesired behavior is immediate, consistent, and positive.

The fix is not to announce the strategy again. It is to change one consequence in the loop. If you want faster decisions, stop rewarding the person who delays by asking for one more data point with extra attention and thorough review. Start requiring a decision within 48 hours and make the default - if no decision is made - the less preferred option. The loop rewires when the consequence changes. Everything else is announcement.

You already have a feedback system. It is running right now, shaping every decision your team makes. The question is not whether to build one - it is whether the one you have is producing the behavior you want, or just the behavior you have.
