Shared quests work only when teaching decisions improve

May 3, 2026 · 5 min read · learning-design, gamification, behaviour-science

In 2011, a gamification startup called Badgeville raised $40 million on the promise that adding points, badges, and leaderboards to enterprise software would transform employee engagement. Within five years, the company pivoted away from gamification entirely. The mechanics were deployed. The behavior did not follow. Badgeville's trajectory is not an outlier - it is the standard outcome when shared quest mechanics land on top of workflows that were never designed to produce better decisions.

Most gamification in professional settings follows a predictable arc: a team identifies a behavior they want more of - course completions, CRM entries, compliance acknowledgments - and wraps it in points, streaks, and leaderboards. Participation spikes for two to six weeks, then decays to baseline or below it. The spike gets reported as success. The decay gets blamed on execution. Deterding and colleagues, who coined the term 'gamification' in their foundational 2011 paper, warned explicitly about this pattern: surface game elements applied without understanding the underlying motivational architecture produce engagement that is brittle, short-lived, and sometimes actively counterproductive.

The failure is not in the mechanics. It is in the teaching decisions that preceded them. A quest in a well-designed game does four things: it specifies what the player should practice, it calibrates difficulty to current skill, it provides feedback that changes the next attempt, and it advances the player into a qualitatively different challenge. When teams deploy shared quests at work, they typically do the first (specify the behavior) and skip the remaining three. The result is a points system layered over a workflow that was never designed to teach anyone anything.

Sailer and Homner's 2020 meta-analysis of gamification in education examined 29 studies and found that gamification's positive effects on motivation were strongest when the design supported autonomy, competence, and relatedness - the three basic psychological needs identified by self-determination theory. When gamification undermined these needs - for instance, by making participation feel compulsory or by publicly ranking people on tasks they had not chosen - motivation decreased relative to no gamification at all. Adding game mechanics to a coercive workflow does not make the workflow engaging. It makes the coercion more visible.

This finding has a direct implication for shared quests in professional settings: if the quest prescribes what to do without offering meaningful choice in how to do it, if the difficulty is uniform rather than calibrated, or if the feedback is a score rather than information that changes the next attempt, the design is actively undermining the conditions under which people learn. The team has not built a quest. They have built a surveillance mechanism with a progress bar.

In game design, failure is engineered. A player dies, respawns, and tries again with slightly more knowledge about what went wrong. The recovery loop - the gap between failure and the next attempt - is where learning happens. Shorten the gap and the player stays engaged. Lengthen it and the player quits. In most workplace gamification, the recovery loop does not exist. A salesperson loses a deal, gets a zero on the leaderboard, and sees their streak reset. There is no structured re-attempt, no diagnostic feedback, no calibrated next challenge. The failure is recorded but not used.

Consider a team that gamifies its onboarding process with a quest structure: complete ten modules, earn a badge, appear on the team leaderboard. A new hire falls behind in week two - the modules are too dense, the examples are irrelevant to their role, and the quiz questions test memorization rather than application. The leaderboard shows them in last place. The streak is broken. The system has now produced a visible signal of inadequacy without providing any mechanism for recovery. Research on gamification in reading instruction - a domain where these mechanics have been heavily tested - consistently finds that self-efficacy, the belief that one can succeed at a specific task, is a stronger predictor of persistence than any extrinsic reward. A recent systematic review of gamification and self-efficacy among language learners confirmed this pattern across multiple study designs: when game mechanics damaged self-efficacy by making failure public and recovery invisible, engagement dropped below pre-gamification baselines. The quest design achieved the opposite of its intent.

Before adding any game mechanic to a workflow, answer four questions about the underlying teaching: What specific decision or judgment should improve after this quest? How will the difficulty adjust based on the participant's current level? What feedback will they receive that changes their next attempt - not a score, but information? And what happens after failure - is there a structured re-engagement, or just a gap on the leaderboard?

Run a simple test: take one recurring team task that already has a completion metric (module finished, form submitted, review completed). Remove the existing metric and replace it with a peer quality check - ask the person who receives the output to rate whether it was usable without revision. Track the no-revision rate across three iterations. If the rate does not improve, the quest mechanic is decoration on a process that does not teach. Fix the teaching first. Add the mechanics second, if at all.

A quest that records failure without reshaping the retry is measuring compliance. Compliance was never the bottleneck - capability was. If the retry looks identical to the first attempt, the design has not taught. It has merely tracked.
