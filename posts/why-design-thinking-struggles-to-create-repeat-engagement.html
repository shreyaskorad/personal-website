Why design thinking struggles to create repeat engagement

May 3, 2026 · 6 min read · modern-l-d, work, learning-design

A global bank in Singapore spent two years embedding design thinking across every operation. Empathy maps, journey maps, rapid prototypes - the full IDEO playbook. The experiences that resulted were cleaner, more intuitive, more inclusive. There was one problem: customers weren't coming back any more often than before.

Design thinking, as popularized by IDEO and now taught in nearly every product school and corporate innovation lab, follows a recognizable arc: empathize with users, define the problem, ideate solutions, prototype, test. It is iterative, collaborative, and broadly effective at what it sets out to do - produce experiences that are intuitive and inclusive. Companies adopt it because it works for its intended purpose.

The trouble starts when teams assume that purpose includes engagement - the sustained, voluntary decision to return to an experience again and again. It doesn't. Design thinking optimizes for usability: can the user accomplish the task with minimal friction? Can they navigate without a manual? These are necessary conditions for a good product. They are not sufficient conditions for a sticky one. A door that opens smoothly on the first visit is not the same as a door people choose to walk through a hundred times.

The distinction matters because teams that adopt design thinking often believe they've also adopted an engagement strategy. They haven't. They've adopted a usability strategy. The empathy mapping uncovered what users need functionally. It did not uncover why users would voluntarily return when that need isn't pressing - when they're tired, distracted, or have twelve competing options for the same task.

The Singapore bank's experience is instructive precisely because it wasn't a failure of execution. The design thinking was implemented thoroughly, with executive sponsorship, cross-functional teams, and genuine organizational commitment. The resulting experiences were measurably better - cleaner interfaces, fewer dead ends, more logical flows. Customer satisfaction scores improved.

But when the bank looked at whether customers were returning more often, engaging more deeply, or choosing its digital products over mobile-first competitors, the numbers barely moved. The gap was structural. Design thinking had improved the surface of the experience. It had not addressed the motivational architecture underneath - the reasons someone chooses one financial app over another when both work fine. The bank needed a framework that could answer a different question: not "can the user complete this task?" but "does the user want to come back tomorrow?".

What the bank eventually found after layering in a behavioral design approach was that the most impactful changes weren't interface improvements at all. They were changes to feedback loops inside the experience - how users saw their progress, what happened right after they completed an action, whether the experience created momentum or simply ended. These are motivational structures, not usability structures. Design thinking, as commonly practiced, doesn't have a vocabulary for them.

The confusion between usability and engagement is pervasive because the two feel identical in the early stages of product development. When a product is genuinely hard to use, improving usability also improves engagement - people return more because they can finally accomplish what they came for. But this convergence is temporary. Once the usability floor is reached, further usability improvements produce diminishing engagement returns. The product works fine. People still leave.

Research from learning science and organizational behavior reinforces this distinction. Studies of how people develop sustained capability - in workplaces, classrooms, or digital products - consistently find that capability alone is necessary but insufficient. Whether people continue a behavior depends on the interaction between capability, opportunity, and motivation. A product that maximizes capability and opportunity while ignoring motivation is building infrastructure for behavior that won't sustain itself. The parallel is exact: usability is capability. Engagement requires all three.

The counterargument is reasonable: isn't usability itself motivating? Doesn't a smooth experience create positive emotion? Yes - but only briefly. Usability reduces friction. It removes negative emotion. It does not generate the positive motivational forces that drive voluntary return - a sense of progress, social connection, meaningful agency, anticipation of something interesting next. Removing the reason someone might leave is not the same as creating a reason someone would stay.

One reason design thinking struggles to produce engagement is that its deliverables are optimized for presentation, not for live behavioral dynamics. A journey map is a document. A persona is a profile. A prototype is a snapshot. These artifacts are genuinely useful for aligning a team around what to build. They are not useful for designing the motivational loops that determine whether the built thing creates return behavior.

Consider what a game designer would call the core loop: the repeating cycle of action, feedback, reward, and next action that keeps a player engaged session after session. Design thinking doesn't naturally produce core loops because its unit of output is the feature or the screen, not the cycle. A team can run a flawless design sprint and emerge with a well-designed checkout flow without ever asking: what happens after checkout that makes the customer want to start the whole cycle again?

The practical consequence is that teams often need a second process after design thinking specifically to address engagement - a retrofit that's more expensive and less effective than designing for motivation from the start. The bank in Singapore spent two years on design thinking before realizing it needed to layer behavioral design on top. The more efficient path is to integrate motivational questions into the same process that produces the user experience, so every feature decision is evaluated against both usability and return behavior simultaneously.

Start with honest diagnosis. Look at your product's return metrics - daily active users, session frequency, time between visits - and ask whether your design process explicitly optimized for any of them. If the process only optimized for task completion and satisfaction scores, your return metrics are accidental, not designed. That's the gap.

Then add one question to every feature decision: does this create a reason to return, or only a reason to stay? Staying is important - low friction and clear navigation matter. But return is a different behavior with different drivers. Features that create reasons to return usually involve some combination of visible progress (the user can see they've moved forward since last time), meaningful choice (the user has agency over what happens next), and social context (the user's actions connect to other people somehow). These are motivational structures, and they can be designed into features rather than bolted on afterward.

Design thinking gets you to usable. That's the entry fee. The harder, more valuable work is designing for the question it was never built to answer: why would someone come back tomorrow? If your process can't answer that, it's not because you're doing it wrong. It's because answering it requires a different design vocabulary - one that treats motivation as architecture, not garnish.
