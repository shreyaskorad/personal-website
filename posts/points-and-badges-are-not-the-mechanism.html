Points and badges are not the mechanism

May 3, 2026 · 4 min read · learning-design, gamification, behaviour-science

A learning platform for medical residents launched a continuing-education module with points for completing case studies, badges for passing quizzes, and a leaderboard showing top performers per cohort. Engagement in the first month was strong: completion rates jumped forty percent compared to the ungamified version. By month three, completion had dropped below the baseline. The residents had not lost interest in the content. They had lost interest in the game - because the game and the content were never the same thing. The points had generated activity. They had not generated learning, and the residents could tell the difference.

Deterding and colleagues, in their 2011 paper defining gamification as the use of game-design elements in non-game contexts, noted that the most common implementations rely on what they called 'pointsification' - adding reward mechanics without redesigning the underlying activity. The result is a thin game layer on top of an experience that was not designed to be engaging in the first place. The points make the activity measurable. They do not make it meaningful.

Sailer and Homner's 2020 meta-analysis examined the conditions under which gamification produces lasting behavior change. They found that the strongest predictor of sustained engagement was not the presence of rewards but the degree to which the experience supported competence - the feeling of getting better at something that matters - and meaningful choice - the sense that your decisions shape the outcome. Points can signal competence, but only if the thing being measured is genuinely challenging and the improvement is real. Badges can acknowledge choice, but only if the choices required to earn them involve genuine trade-offs rather than compliance.

The medical residents stopped engaging not because the points disappeared but because the points were measuring completion, not clinical reasoning. Completing a case study with a badge attached is not the same as working through a diagnostic puzzle where the answer is not obvious and the consequences of getting it wrong matter. The points system measured the wrong thing - activity instead of thinking - and once the novelty wore off, the residents noticed.

A fair defense: points and badges serve a legitimate purpose as onboarding. They lower the barrier to first engagement. A resident who would not voluntarily open a case study module might open one if there is a point incentive. Once they are in, the content can do the work. The points are the door, not the room.

The problem with this defense is the evidence on motivational crowding. Decades of research on self-determination theory, beginning with Deci and Ryan's work in the 1980s and extending through Sailer and Homner's meta-analysis, show that introducing extrinsic rewards for an activity that could be intrinsically motivating can undermine the intrinsic motivation itself. The mechanism is not additive - extrinsic rewards do not stack on top of intrinsic engagement. They can replace it. When the points stop, the engagement stops too, even if the underlying experience was designed to be compelling. The points do not get people in the door. They teach people that the reason to walk through the door is the points, not what is inside the room.

A more productive approach: before adding any reward mechanic, ask what you want people to be doing six months from now when the novelty is gone and no one is handing out badges anymore. If the answer is 'completing modules,' points will work - completion is easy to measure and reward. If the answer is 'making better clinical decisions under pressure,' points will not work, because clinical reasoning under pressure requires practice with genuine friction, ambiguous cases, and feedback that addresses the quality of thinking rather than the fact of completion.

To test whether your engagement design is building toward intrinsic motivation: remove the points and badges for one cohort. If engagement drops to zero, the points were the entire mechanism and the underlying experience was not worth doing. If engagement drops but stabilizes - people continue because the cases are interesting and the feedback helps them think - the points were scaffolding that can be removed once the intrinsic engagement is established. Track the engagement level of the no-points cohort at four weeks and again at twelve weeks. If it is within fifteen percent of the gamified cohort at twelve weeks, the experience itself is holding. If it is below fifty percent, the points were never scaffolding - they were the whole structure, and the structure disappears when the rewards do.

Points measure what happened. They do not cause what happens next. If the experience cannot hold attention without them, adding them only delays the moment you discover the design was never the mechanism.
