Why delight belongs inside the core loop

May 3, 2026 · 4 min read · modern-l-d, work, learning-design

Dyson doesn't benchmark its robot vacuums against Electrolux or Bosch. It benchmarks them against hiring a person to clean your house. That comparison reframes the entire product: the robot should recommend which room to start with, adjust its approach based on the floor type, and make you proud to display it in your entrance hall. A vacuum cleaner that generates pride is doing something beyond function. It has crossed into emotional territory - and that crossing happened because the team chose a different comparison point, not because they added confetti.

Most product teams benchmark against direct competitors: which features they have, which gaps exist, where the parity breaks. This produces a specific kind of product - one that matches the market's expectations and nothing more. The emotional dimension never enters the frame because competitors don't expose it in a feature matrix.

Dyson's approach is different. By comparing their robot to a human cleaner, they introduced questions that no competitor comparison would generate: does the product recommend which room needs attention first? Does it adapt its behavior to the situation? Does it make the owner feel proud enough to display it publicly? Each of these questions has an emotional component baked into the functional one. The product either generates that feeling or it doesn't - and the benchmark makes that binary visible.

The principle generalizes. When a product team compares its offering to the best human version of the same service, emotional requirements surface automatically. A banking app compared to a personal financial advisor needs to anticipate needs, not just display balances. A learning platform compared to a great tutor needs to create moments of surprise and recognition, not just deliver content. The comparison point does the work of surfacing emotional requirements that feature parity hides.

At Google, the Chrome mobile team confronted a technical constraint: users were accumulating thousands of open browser tabs, which degraded performance. The functional fix was obvious - auto-close old tabs, compress the grid, limit the count. Instead the team sat with users and watched them navigate their tab collections. What they found was not a navigation problem but an emotional one.

Users apologized for their clutter. They said things like 'sorry, I usually don't have so many open.' They described their tabs with possessive language - 'don't you dare touch my tabs.' The team realized that closing tabs automatically would violate a relationship, not just remove data. The feature they built grouped tabs inactive for more than 21 days into a separate section. The tabs remained accessible but the main view showed only active ones. Performance improved. The shame vanished. One mechanism addressed both the functional and emotional dimensions simultaneously.

Research on training transfer by Grossman and Salas identifies a parallel pattern in workplace learning: the post-training environment determines whether new capabilities survive or decay. Formal instruction matters less than the conditions that greet the learner when they return to their desk. The Chrome team applied this instinctively - they didn't just fix the technical problem, they redesigned the environment the user returns to. The emotional context of the tab grid was the post-training environment, and they restructured it so that the desired behavior (feeling in control) could survive.

Revolut, the banking app built originally for currency exchange, discovered that its core users were international travelers. Instead of adding features competitors already had, the team anticipated what travelers would need next. The result: an eSIM available directly inside the banking app - something nobody expects from their bank. The feature works functionally (you get connectivity abroad) and emotionally (your bank just solved a problem you hadn't articulated yet). That's delight through anticipation, not decoration.

The distinction matters because anticipation creates a different design brief than satisfaction. Satisfaction means you gave people what they asked for. Anticipation means you identified what they would need before they asked. The first produces contentment. The second produces the kind of surprise that converts users into advocates. The metric difference is visible: support tickets about a frustrating flow should drop measurably - aim for 15% within one sprint cycle - and the language users employ in feedback sessions should shift from complaints to unprompted positive mentions.

The review checkpoint is straightforward: after shipping a feature, replay the same user research format that exposed the emotional friction initially. If users stop apologizing, stop complaining, or start describing the experience in positive terms without prompting, the emotional architecture is holding. If the language hasn't shifted, the feature addressed function without touching feeling - and the gap will widen over time as competitors match the functional baseline.

Every product makes people feel something. The question is whether that feeling was designed or accidental. Dyson chose to benchmark against a person, not a competitor, and pride became a requirement. Chrome chose to watch users apologize before redesigning the grid, and shame became the design target. Revolut chose to anticipate what travelers needed before they asked, and surprise became the standard. In each case, delight wasn't added - it was discovered by asking a different question first.
